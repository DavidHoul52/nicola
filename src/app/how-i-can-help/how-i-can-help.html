What is CBT?

Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) is a practical, collaborative therapy. It looks at how our thoughts, feelings, body and behaviour affect one another, and how patterns that once made sense can keep difficulties going now.

Alongside what is happening in the present, we also make sense of where those patterns came from. Then, together, we try out new ways of relating to yourself, others and the world.

How CBT can help

Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT) is a collaborative process. You are the expert on your own experiences, while I bring my therapeutic knowledge and experience. We work together to develop an understanding of the difficulties you’re experiencing and what might be keeping them going.

We begin by exploring patterns in how you think, feel, behave and experience sensations in your body, as well as how past experiences may have shaped these patterns. Some of the ways we have learnt to think and cope may have served us well at one time, but may no longer feel helpful or work in the way they once did. Developing an understanding of these patterns can help us identify new ways of responding that better support you.

Bringing CBT to life: an example of low self-esteem

Imagine making a mistake at work or receiving feedback that you find difficult to hear. This might trigger a familiar pattern of responses:

  • Thoughts: You might find yourself thinking, “I’m not good enough”, “I’ve let everyone down” or “I’m going to get found out”.
  • Feelings: You might experience sadness, anxiety, shame or embarrassment.
  • Body sensations: You might notice tension in your shoulders, a knot in your stomach, a racing heart or a feeling of heaviness and exhaustion.
  • Behaviours: You might withdraw from others, avoid new challenges, over-prepare, seek reassurance or become particularly self-critical.

These different responses are interconnected and can reinforce one another over time, strengthening the belief that you’re not good enough and influencing how you see yourself and what you feel able to do.

How might we work with this?

Together, we would explore where these patterns may have come from, what tends to trigger them and how they are maintained. We might consider how earlier experiences have shaped the way you see yourself, and whether the beliefs and ways of coping you’ve developed are still serving you. We can then begin to gently question unhelpful patterns, develop a more balanced and compassionate view of yourself, and experiment with different ways of responding. I’m also interested in the ways emotional difficulties and relationship patterns interact. Depression, anxiety, trauma, low self-worth and difficult life events can effect how we relate to others, while relationship difficulties can leave us feeling isolated, overwhelmed and stuck.

An evidence-based approach

CBT is an evidence-based approach, meaning it has been extensively researched and shown to be effective in helping people manage a range of emotional and psychological difficulties. It is recommended by the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) in the UK for a number of mental health conditions.

My approach

A particular interest of mine is the way our emotional lives and our relationships shape each other. In CBT we look closely at the patterns that keep difficulties going in the present, and make sense of how they first developed. From there, we work on new ways of responding — to yourself, to the people around you and to life more broadly — so you can meet things with more kindness, confidence and flexibility.

I draw on a range of therapeutic approaches rather than believing that one model has all the answers.
