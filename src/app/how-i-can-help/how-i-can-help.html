What is CBT?

Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT) is a practical, collaborative therapy that explores how our thoughts, feelings, body sensations and behaviours influence one another. It helps us understand patterns that contribute to emotional difficulties and find new ways of responding.

Alongside exploring what’s happening in the present, we may also consider how earlier experiences have shaped the way we see ourselves, others and the world. Often, the ways we’ve learnt to think and cope made sense in the circumstances we were in, even if they no longer serve us well. Understanding this can help us approach ourselves with greater kindness and less self-criticism.

An evidence-based approach

CBT is extensively researched and has been shown to be effective in helping people manage a range of emotional and psychological difficulties. It is recommended by the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) in the UK for a number of mental health conditions.

How CBT can help

CBT is a collaborative process. You are the expert on your own experiences, while I bring my therapeutic knowledge and experience. Together, we develop an understanding of your difficulties and what might help.

We explore how you think, feel, behave and experience sensations in your body, as well as the situations and experiences that influence these responses. This can help us recognise what keeps difficulties going and identify opportunities for change.

Bringing CBT to life: an example of low self-esteem

Imagine making a mistake at work or receiving difficult feedback. This might trigger a familiar pattern:

  • Thoughts: “I’m not good enough”, “I’ve let everyone down” or “I’m going to get found out”.
  • Feelings: Sadness, anxiety, shame or embarrassment.
  • Body sensations: Tension, a knot in your stomach, a racing heart or exhaustion.
  • Behaviours: Withdrawing, avoiding new challenges, over-preparing, seeking reassurance or becoming self-critical.

These responses are interconnected and can reinforce one another, strengthening the belief that you’re not good enough and affecting how you see yourself and what you feel able to do.

How might we work with this?

Together, we would explore what triggers these patterns and what keeps them going. We might look at the beliefs you hold about yourself, how they developed and whether there are other ways of understanding a situation.

We can then begin to gently question unhelpful patterns, develop a more balanced and compassionate view of yourself, and experiment with different ways of responding.

I’m also interested in how emotional difficulties and relationship patterns interact. Depression, anxiety, trauma, low self-worth and difficult life events can affect how we relate to others, while relationship difficulties can leave us feeling isolated, overwhelmed and stuck.

My approach

A particular interest of mine is the way our emotional lives and our relationships shape each other. In CBT we look closely at the patterns that keep difficulties going in the present, and make sense of how they first developed. From there, we work on new ways of responding — to yourself, to the people around you and to life more broadly — so you can meet things with more kindness, confidence and flexibility.

I draw on a range of therapeutic approaches rather than believing that one model has all the answers.
